# Papyrus 52 and Papyrus 75 — Early New Testament Manuscripts

## What They Are

**Papyrus 52 (𝔓52)** and **Papyrus 75 (𝔓75)** are two of the earliest known Greek manuscripts of the New Testament. They are written on papyrus in **codex** form (the ancestor of the modern book) and contain portions of the Gospels of John and Luke.

## Papyrus 52 (Rylands Papyrus)

- A credit-card-sized fragment from the **Gospel of John** (18:31–33 and 18:37–38), held at the John Rylands Library in Manchester.
- Traditionally dated to **c. 100–150 C.E.**; recent paleographic reassessments propose a broader range of **c. 125–225 C.E.** or later.
- Written in Greek on both sides of the papyrus, proving it came from a codex, not a scroll.
- It is the earliest surviving fragment of a canonical New Testament text.

## Papyrus 75 (Bodmer Papyrus XIV–XV)

- Contains most of **Luke 3:18–24:53** and **John 1:1–15:8**.
- Housed today in the **Vatican Library** (Hanna Papyrus 1, “Mater Verbi”).
- Traditionally dated to the **late 2nd to early 3rd century C.E.** (c. 175–225); some recent scholars argue for a range extending into the 4th century.
- Its text is remarkably close to **Codex Vaticanus**, one of the most important manuscripts of the Greek New Testament.

## Why They Matter

- 𝔓52 shows that the Gospel of John was being copied and circulated **within decades of the events it records** (and possibly within the lifetime of eyewitnesses).
- 𝔓75 demonstrates textual stability: a 2nd/3rd-century papyrus already agrees closely with a major 4th-century uncial manuscript.
- Together they support the conclusion that the New Testament text was transmitted carefully from a very early period.

## KJV Connection

- The **KJV New Testament** was translated from the **Textus Receptus**, a printed edition based mainly on later Byzantine manuscripts.
- 𝔓52 and 𝔓75 represent the **Alexandrian text-type**, which differs from the Byzantine tradition in many places.
- Textual scholars use these early papyri — along with Codex Vaticanus, Codex Sinaiticus, and others — to evaluate readings behind the KJV.

## Notes

- Dating early papyri by handwriting alone is debated; scholars give ranges rather than single years.
- These manuscripts do not “prove” the KJV text word-for-word, but they do show that the Gospel tradition was fixed and copied very early.
- For KJV-related study, the main takeaway is the antiquity and relative textual stability of the New Testament manuscript tradition.
