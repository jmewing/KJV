## Artifact Summary

- **Artifact:** Tel Dan Stele (fragmentary basalt stela)
- **Date:** Generally 9th century B.C.E. (870–750 B.C.E.)
- **Discovered:** 1993–94 at Tel Dan, northern Israel
- **Excavator:** Avraham Biran; inscription read by Joseph Naveh
- **Current location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem
- **Language:** Old Aramaic in Phoenician script
- **Biblical connection:** 2 Kings 8–9; 2 Chronicles 22 (Jehoram of Israel, Ahaziah of Judah, Hazael of Damascus)

## Key Points

- The stele records the victory of an Aramean king (most likely Hazael of Damascus) over the "king of Israel" and the "king of the House of David."
- It contains the phrase **ביתדוד** (bytdwd), "House of David," the earliest widely accepted extra-biblical mention of David and the Davidic dynasty.
- It is one of only four known extra-biblical inscriptions from c. 1200–800 B.C.E. containing the name "Israel" — the others are the Merneptah Stele, the Mesha Stele, and the Kurkh Monoliths.
- The inscription aligns with 2 Kings 8–9: Hazael's rise and the deaths of Jehoram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah.
- Some minimalists dispute whether bytdwd means a dynastic "House of David" rather than a place name, but the dynastic reading is accepted by the large majority of scholars.

## What It Corroborates

- King David was a real historical figure whose dynasty was recognized by neighboring nations over a century after his death.
- The kingdoms of Israel and Judah existed as separate but related political entities in the 9th century B.C.E.
- The biblical account of Hazael's campaigns in 2 Kings fits the historical record of an Aramean king boasting of victories in the Levant.
