# The Caiaphas Ossuary

**Source:** Wikipedia + Israel Museum + Biblical Archaeology Society  
**URLs:**

## Artifact Summary

- **Artifact:** Ossuary (limestone bone box) of Joseph son of Caiaphas
- **Date:** 1st century C.E. (Second Temple period)
- **Discovered:** December 1990 in a burial cave in south Jerusalem (Peace Forest / North Talpiot area)
- **Current location:** Israel Museum, Jerusalem
- **Language:** Aramaic/Hebrew in Jewish script
- **Biblical connection:** Caiaphas, high priest who presided over Jesus’ Sanhedrin trial (Matthew 26:57–68; John 11:49–53; 18:13–14)

## Key Points

- The ossuary is one of twelve found in a family burial cave; two bore the name Caiaphas.
- The ornate ossuary is inscribed twice: "Yehosef bar Qayafa" and "Yehosef bar Qafa" — "Joseph son of Caiaphas."
- It contained the bones of a roughly 60-year-old male, two teenage boys, an adult woman, and two infants.
- A later, looted ossuary (the Miriam ossuary) was recovered in 2011 and is inscribed: "Miriam daughter of Yeshua son of Caiaphas, Priest of Ma’aziah from Beth ‘Imri," possibly linking the family to the priestly course of Ma’aziah.

## What It Corroborates

- The personal name and family of Caiaphas, previously known mainly from the New Testament and Josephus, are attested archaeologically.
- High-priestly families used inscribed ossuaries in rock-cut tombs around Jerusalem in the early 1st century C.E.
- The find is consistent with the Gospel accounts placing Caiaphas as a leading religious authority in Jerusalem at the time of Jesus.

## Status

Most scholars accept the ossuary as authentic and plausibly belonging to the high priest Caiaphas, though some debate continues over spelling details and whether the inscription explicitly identifies him as high priest.
